The State of Remote Work in 2024

Remote work has fundamentally reshaped how organizations operate in the modern world. According to a 2024 Stanford study, 42% of U.S. workers now perform their jobs remotely at least part of the time. This shift has prompted companies to rethink everything from office leases to performance management. Furthermore, the transition has not been uniform across industries, and the long-term implications remain a subject of ongoing debate.

I started working remotely in 2019, before the broader transition took hold. Back then, my small team synced up over Slack and Zoom, and we shipped a logistics platform used by a couple of major carriers. The post-pandemic wave of remote work, however, is a different animal entirely. The Stanford researchers tracked 12,400 employees across 38 firms between January 2022 and June 2024, and their findings contradict a lot of the venture-capital-fueled return-to-office push.

Productivity, measured by completed tickets and shipped features, rose 9% for fully-remote workers and 13% for hybrid workers compared to their in-office peers. Moreover, attrition fell by a third over the same period. That said, the picture isn't uniformly rosy. Onboarding new hires is more challenging, and the watercooler serendipity that produces unexpected collaborations is mostly gone. A 2023 internal memo, leaked to the press that August, found that engineers hired post-2020 took 31% longer to reach ramp.

The strategies that play a crucial role tend to share a few common features. First, asynchronous communication is essential for distributed teams. Teams that default to written updates outperform teams that default to meetings. The research group measured this directly: teams with more than 60% of communication happening asynchronously had 22% higher output. Additionally, this requires deliberate investment in documentation culture and the discipline to maintain it over time.

Second, intentional in-person time matters more than many organizations initially assumed. The companies that scored highest on employee satisfaction weren't fully-remote; they were hybrid with deliberate quarterly gatherings. The cost of such gatherings is significant, but the payoff in trust scores is substantial. Third, clear working hours are vital for sustained productivity. Remote workers who set explicit availability windows reported 41% less burnout than those who stayed always on. This sounds obvious in theory, but it is not how most remote workers actually behave in practice.

There are real concerns that warrant attention. Middle management has hollowed out — a recent McKinsey report from Q1 2024 estimated that 18% of manager roles in tech have been de-layered since 2021. Junior employees report feeling invisible. The impact on commercial real estate is now impossible to ignore; one major market's office vacancy rate hit 35.6% in March 2024. Furthermore, these trends suggest that the remote-work transition is producing second-order effects that organizations are still learning to navigate.

Furthermore, the case for forced return-to-office, five days a week, is weak. The productivity data doesn't support it. The attrition data actively undermines it. The talent market — especially for engineers and designers — will punish companies that insist on it. It is worth noting that the companies loudest about return-to-office are often those with the longest lease commitments and the most sunk cost in physical infrastructure.

In conclusion, my own take, having done this for five years across two startups, is that remote work is not a perk or a policy but a different operating model altogether. Treating it like the office but on Zoom is the mistake most companies make. The teams that thrive are the ones that rebuild their norms from scratch, investing in documentation, asynchronous communication, and intentional in-person collaboration.

The 2024 numbers are not a blip, and the underlying trends appear durable. Whether the five-day return-to-office holdouts hold the line through 2025 is the open question. The data, at least, is not on their side.
